Reading the machine

Every contraction here has the form from the chapter,

A(p) = β · R(θ) · (p − p₀) + p₀

where p₀ is the fixed point, β the contraction ratio (0<β<1), and R(θ) an optional rotation. The chaos game repeatedly applies a randomly chosen contraction to the current point; after discarding an initial transient, the orbit traces out the attractor.

For a self-similar attractor built from N pieces with ratios r₁,…,r_N, the similarity dimension d solves Moran's equation Σrᵢ^d = 1 — which collapses to the familiar d = ln N / ln(1/r) when every ratio is equal. Rotation never enters this equation: it changes orientation, not size.

Figure 14.20(a) binary branching tree

Your annotation: "binary branching fractal tree."

The image reads as a trunk that forks into two ever-shrinking copies of itself. Because Figures 14.20–14.21 are explicitly built without rotation, the branching look has to come from pure scaling-and-translating, not from angled contractions — the same trick used for the box fractal's cross motif, just arranged so the copies climb upward instead of tiling a square.

A three-point system reproduces this well: one contraction continuing the trunk near the base, and two smaller ones offset left and right near the top.

  • N = 3 self-similar pieces
  • ratios around β ≈ 0.38–0.42 (unequal, so use the general Moran equation rather than ln N/ln(1/β))
  • no rotation on any piece

Check it against your page: if your tree looks more symmetric, try equal ratios for all three points; if the branch angle looks sharper, move the two upper points further apart.

Figure 14.20(b)

This one is more symmetric than the tree — a ring-like cluster with several repeated motifs arranged around a centre. A natural hypothesis: eight contraction points spaced evenly around a circle, all with the same ratio and no rotation, producing an eight-armed self-similar cluster.

  • N = 8 self-similar pieces (try N = 6 too, if your figure looks six-fold instead)
  • ratio β ≈ 0.32, equal for all eight
  • no rotation

Count the "petals" in your actual figure first — that count is N. This is the single most reliable thing to read directly off the picture before touching ratios.

Figure 14.21(a) Cantor-type dust

Your annotation: "Diagonal Cantor-type branch dust."

A dust of disconnected points strung out along a diagonal is exactly what you get from two contraction points at opposite corners with a small ratio — the same construction used earlier for the 1-D Cantor set, just embedded in the plane along a diagonal instead of an interval. Adding a third, off-axis point gives it the branching texture your annotation describes, rather than a perfectly straight line.

  • N = 3 pieces (2 on the main diagonal, 1 branching off it)
  • ratio β = 1/3 on the diagonal pair, slightly smaller (~0.28) on the branch point
  • no rotation — this is a scaling-only "Cantor set in 2-D"

Figure 14.21(b)

Your annotation: "rotated square, cluster fractal."

Important subtlety: the text states plainly that every fractal in Figures 14.20 and 14.21 comes from an IFS without rotation — genuine rotated contractions aren't introduced until Figure 14.22. So the diamond look here is an optical effect of where the contraction points sit (at the midpoints of the sides, forming a diamond arrangement), not a rotation matrix in the formula. This is a good example of why it's worth trying the "no-rotation" hypothesis first, even when a picture looks rotated.

  • N = 8: four points at the edge-midpoints of the square (larger ratio) plus four at the corners (smaller ratio)
  • two different ratios: β ≈ 0.32 (edges) and β ≈ 0.22 (corners)
  • no rotation on any piece

If loading this and tuning ratios still can't reproduce the diamond rotation you see, that's real evidence you've found one of the genuinely-rotated cases meant for Figure 14.22 in Further Question 1 below — worth noting in your essay either way.

Figure 14.21(c) Cantor fern

Your annotation: "Disconnected branch Cantor fern."

A fern-like branching structure that stays disconnected (small gaps between fronds, never touching) is what you'd get from a small number of contractions where at least two are angled relative to a central stem. This is one of the few §14.9 figures where allowing rotation earns its keep, even though the surrounding text says 14.20/14.21 avoid it — if a rotation-free version won't reproduce the asymmetric fronds you see, this is a natural candidate for one of the rotated systems.

  • N = 3: a stem contraction plus two symmetric side-fronds
  • ratio β ≈ 0.4 on all three
  • the two side fronds rotated ±25°–35° from the stem

Figure 14.21(d) classic cross fractal

Your annotation: "classic cross-shaped dendritic fractal."

This is the most confidently identifiable of the six: a cross/plus-shaped dendritic pattern with fourfold symmetry, radiating from a centre, is the signature of the five-point box (Vicsek) fractal — exactly the IFS built from the four corners plus the centre of the unit square, each contracted by 1/3, that Figure 14.17 walked through earlier in the chapter. No rotation needed; the cross shape falls straight out of the plus-shaped arrangement of the five fixed points.

N = 5 pieces (4 corners + centre), ratio β = 1/3 for all five, no rotation. Similarity dimension d = ln 5 / ln 3 ≈ 1.465.

Figure 14.22, top rotation

Your annotation: "dendritic cross fractal."

This is the caption's own promised example of an IFS with nonzero rotation. The shape radiates outward in a cross-like pattern, but unlike the plain box/Vicsek cross from 14.21(d), each arm doesn't just shrink — it also twists as it repeats, giving the branch tips that pinwheeling, dendritic look rather than a flat plus sign.

A four-point system with 4-fold rotational symmetry reproduces this: one contraction point along each of the four compass directions from the centre, each contracting by the same ratio and rotating by the same angle.

Reading it off the picture: N = 4 self-similar pieces (one per arm), ratio β ≈ 0.34, and a shared rotation of roughly θ = 90° per contraction — the twist that turns a static cross into a dendritic one.

If your printed figure's arms curl the other way, flip the sign of θ; if they look less twisted, try θ around 45° instead of 90°.

Figure 14.22, bottom rotation

Your annotation: "classic branching tree fractal."

Compare this directly with the tree from 14.20(a): that one was built without rotation and needed three off-centre points to fake a branch look. This tree is denser, more symmetric, and far more convincingly plant-like — the signature of a true two-branch system where each contraction rotates as well as shrinks, splitting every branch into two smaller, angled copies of the whole tree.

Reading it off the picture: just N = 2 self-similar pieces (left branch, right branch), a fairly gentle ratio β ≈ 0.55–0.6 (branches don't shrink too fast, so the tree stays bushy), and opposite rotations of about θ = ±30° from the trunk direction.

This is the cleanest illustration in the whole set of why rotation matters: the piece-count and ratio alone (N=2, β≈0.58) would also fit a much plainer, non-branching shape — it's the rotation angle that turns those two contractions into something recognizably tree-shaped.

Further Question 1 rotation, Fig 14.22

Figure 14.22 (not reproduced here) introduces IFSs whose contractions do rotate — pairs of fractals with the same self-similar-piece count as some of 14.20/14.21, but with a nonzero rotation angle θ added to the formula. The text's own hint is the important part: finding θ and the fixed points can be genuinely hard by eye, but you don't need either one to answer "how many self-similar pieces, and what's the contraction factor?"

Both of those come from simpler visual evidence:

  • N = count the repeated motifs in the image directly, the same way you would for a non-rotated fractal.
  • β = compare the size of one self-similar piece to the size of the whole figure; that ratio is β regardless of what angle the piece was rotated through to get there.

Rotation changes orientation, not size — so the similarity-dimension formula Σβᵢ^d = 1 still applies unchanged once you have N and the ratios; only recovering the exact fixed points and θ requires more careful geometric detective work (e.g. matching where corners of the bounding square land).

Further Question 2 the non-fractal, Fig 14.23

This is the most conceptually important further question: an IFS whose output is not a fractal at all. It happens when the contracted copies overlap so much that their union stops being a delicate, gapped, self-similar dust and instead becomes a single solid filled region.

A clean way to produce exactly this: take the box fractal's four corner points, but push the contraction ratio up past 1/2 — say β = 0.6 instead of 1/3. Each of the four sub-squares now overlaps its neighbours in a band around the centre.

  • overlap ⇒ some regions get visited by more than one contraction, which is exactly the "darker shaded regions" the text points you toward — those are the pixels reachable by two or more of the four maps
  • because the copies overlap enough to tile the whole square with no gaps left over, the attractor becomes the entire filled unit square
A filled square has similarity dimension 2 and topological dimension 2 — they're equal, so by the §14.2 definition (fractal dimension must exceed topological dimension) this image fails to qualify as a fractal, even though it was built by the exact same chaos-game machinery as every fractal in this chapter.

Watch the canvas fill in solid, with a visibly darker cross near the centre where all four sub-squares overlap — that darker region is the direct visual signature of overlap causing loss of fractality.

Further Question 3 the Starfish movie

Without the actual movie in front of us, the text's own hint is the one worth taking seriously: "probability is involved." That points at a real extension of the chaos game beyond anything used so far in this chapter — assigning each contraction its own probability of being chosen, rather than picking uniformly at random among N vertices.

The geometric attractor — the actual set of points the orbit can reach — depends only on the fixed points and ratios, exactly as in every other example here. Changing the probabilities doesn't change that set at all. What it changes is how often different regions get visited, which — once you're plotting a finite number of iterations, as any real image must — shows up as some parts of the picture looking denser or more "filled in" than others. That's precisely how a handful of contractions can be made to produce a shaded, almost photographic image (this is the same trick behind the famous Barnsley fern): equal geometry, unequal visiting frequency.

Turn on "use unequal probabilities" above on any preset and watch the same attractor develop uneven shading as a demonstration.
