A simple random process that surprisingly produces the Sierpinski triangle — a classic fractal.

🌀

This is called the Chaos Game — a random dynamical system.

📐

Rules of the game

  1. Pick some points in the plane — the vertices of a shape.
  2. Pick any starting point p₀ — it doesn't even need to be inside the shape.
🎲

Iterate

  • Randomly choose one vertex.
  • Move partway toward it (halfway, by default).
  • That new point becomes the next in the sequence.
  • Repeat forever: p₀ → p₁ → p₂ → …
🔭

Orbit & the big question

This sequence is called the orbit of p₀. If you always pick the same vertex, the orbit marches straight there and stops. But what happens if the choice is genuinely random, every time? Play below and find out — then start bending the rules.

🔍 What's actually going on

Why "always the same vertex" converges

Moving halfway toward a fixed point is a contraction — it always shrinks the distance to that point by the same fraction. Do it forever and you're squeezed onto that single point, no matter where you started.

Why true randomness builds a fractal

At every step, huge regions become impossible to land in — no matter which vertex you move toward, you can never jump into certain "forbidden" zones from outside them. What's left after infinitely many steps is the delicate, gapped Sierpinski triangle.

Why the square just fills in solid

With 4 corners and ratio 1/2, the four shrunken copies of the square tile it perfectly — no gaps, no overlaps. There's no "forbidden zone" left to carve out a fractal, so the whole square fills in.

Why banning repeats brings a fractal back

Forbidding a repeat removes some of the moves that were filling in those gaps. That's enough to open up new forbidden zones — and a fractal reappears, even in shapes that looked solid under plain randomness.
