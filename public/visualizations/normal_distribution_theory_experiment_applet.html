Normal Distribution

From real-world measurements → centre & spread → bell curve → probability

Why do we need the Normal Distribution?

Many measurements cluster around an average.
How can we describe their centre, spread and probabilities?

Imagine measuring the heights of many students. Most observations tend to lie near the average, while fewer observations occur far away from it.

  • Where is the centre of the data?
  • How much spread is there around the mean?
  • What proportion of observations lies between two values?

The Normal Distribution provides a smooth, symmetric model for many naturally occurring measurements and for the behaviour of sample means under common conditions.

From data to a distribution

1. Collect data
Measure heights, scores, errors, etc.
2. Find the centre
Mean μ
3. Measure spread
Standard deviation σ
4. Model the data
X ~ Normal(μ, σ²)
For a normal random variable X, the distribution is determined by two parameters:
Mean μ = centre    |    Standard deviation σ = spread

🎯 A concrete example

Exam scores: Suppose scores are approximately Normal with mean 70 and standard deviation 10.

μ = 70,   σ = 10,   X = exam score

We can ask: What fraction of students score between 60 and 80? What is the probability of a score above 90?

The same ideas are useful for measurement errors, biological measurements, manufacturing dimensions, test scores, sensor noise and many other settings.

📘 The Normal Model

A Normal distribution is continuous, symmetric and bell-shaped. Its location and spread are determined by:

  1. μ is the mean and locates the centre of the curve.
  2. σ is the standard deviation and controls the width.
  3. The total area under the density curve is 1.
  4. The curve is symmetric about x = μ.
  5. For a continuous variable, probability corresponds to area under the curve.
f(x)= 1/(σ√(2π)) · exp[−(x−μ)²/(2σ²)]

🔎 The z-score: standardising a value

z = (x − μ) / σ

A z-score tells us how many standard deviations a value is above or below the mean.

📏 The 68–95–99.7 rule

For a Normal distribution, approximately 68% of observations lie within 1σ of μ, 95% within 2σ, and 99.7% within 3σ.
